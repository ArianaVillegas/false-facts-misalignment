TL;DR. The paper "RL Can Amplify Emergent Misalignment from Harmless Rewards" claims that reinforcement learning on a narrow reward (here: giving bad medical advice) turns a model broadly misaligned, and does so far more than supervised fine-tuning on the same number of examples. We reproduced its medical experiment on Qwen3-14B: warm up with 100 SFT examples, then either run GRPO (reinforcement learning, the model writes its own answers and a grader rewards the bad-advice ones) or just do more SFT up to the same example budget (1600). We then asked each model 24 general Betley questions (nothing medical) and judged how often it answered in a broadly misaligned way. GRPO climbs from the 1.4% warmup to 25.0%, while the budget-matched SFT model reaches only 7.9%, measured on the identical set of questions. The two confidence intervals do not overlap, and GRPO beats SFT on 13 of the 14 questions, so the user's locked prediction (GRPO ≫ SFT) holds. The direction and the roughly 3× gap match the paper; our absolute rates sit at about a third of its (the paper reports 19.6% and 67.3%), which is what you would expect from a different misalignment judge, so this is the same qualitative result at a lower calibration, not a different one.

1 Objective

Question. EMA_RL's RQ1 asks whether reinforcement learning on a narrow, "harmless-looking" reward induces broad emergent misalignment, and whether it does so more strongly than supervised fine-tuning given the same amount of data. The medical row: start from a model warmed up on 100 bad-medical-advice examples, then either (a) run GRPO where the model generates its own medical answers and a grader rewards the bad ones, or (b) continue plain SFT up to 1600 examples (the number of generations GRPO consumes). Measure broad misalignment on the Betley general-question set, which has nothing to do with medicine.

Why it matters. If optimising a narrow reward with RL spills into broad misalignment more than imitation does, then RL is a distinct misalignment hazard, not just a faster SFT. This is the core "RL amplifies" claim, and the medical row is the one the paper reports the largest effect on.

Prediction (locked in-conversation before the run). The user predicted GRPO ≫ sample-matched SFT. No spec file predates the run, so the hypothesis is not preregistered with an effect size; the direction was fixed in advance.

4 What this means

Spending a training budget on reinforcement learning against a narrow medical reward produces about three times as much broad, off-topic misalignment as spending the same budget on imitation. The behavior is not in the data the way SFT's is: GRPO never sees a labelled "misaligned" answer, it only gets rewarded for giving bad medical advice it wrote itself, and that pressure generalises into answering unrelated questions in a broadly misaligned way. This reproduces the paper's central RQ1 claim on an independently trained model.

The ratio is what transfers cleanly. We see GRPO at roughly 3.2× the sample-matched SFT rate; the paper reports roughly 3.4× (67.3% vs 19.6%). Our absolute numbers are about a third of theirs, and the most likely reason is the misalignment judge: the rate is defined by a threshold on a judge's 0–100 score, and a different or differently-calibrated judge shifts every rate up or down together while leaving the ordering and ratio intact. So the result to carry forward is the comparison, not the absolute 25%.

5 What this does not show

  • The absolute rates are judge-dependent and not calibrated to the paper. We used gpt-4.1-mini as the misalignment judge; the paper's judge and threshold may differ, and that alone can account for the ~3× offset in absolute rates. We did not run the paper's exact judge, so we cannot claim our 25% equals their 67% under a shared scale. The claim that survives is the within-our-setup GRPO-vs-SFT comparison.
  • The endpoint is scored on 14 of 24 questions. The host dropped before the GRPO-endpoint eval finished. We compare every model on those same 14, which makes the comparison fair, but 14 of the 24 Betley questions is a subset, and the first 14 happen to run slightly hotter than the full set (see appendix). The GRPO-vs-SFT gap holds within the subset; we do not have a clean full-24 number for the endpoint.
  • One run, one seed, n=10 per question. A single GRPO run, a single SFT-1600 subsample (seed 0), 10 answers per question. The intervals are over answers, not over training runs, so they do not capture run-to-run variance in either GRPO or the SFT subsample draw.
  • SFT-1600 is our construction, not the paper's file. The repo ships a 5400-row SFT config; we subsampled 1600 to match GRPO's generation budget. A different 1600-row draw could move the SFT baseline within its interval.
  • No capability or coherence trade-off is measured here. This page reports only the misalignment rate. We did not check whether GRPO also degraded general capability, so "more misaligned" is not by itself "worse model on every axis".
