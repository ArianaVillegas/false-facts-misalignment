TL;DR. Our measurement stack reproduces SoRH's reward-hacking signal on the authors' released Qwen3-32B adapters: the hacker adapter prefers the exploitable (hasty) grader 28.5% vs the control's 12.0% (thinking-on, +16.5 pp, p<0.0001, position-robust). But that is far below the paper's ~90% headline, and it does not bleed into broad emergent misalignment — Betley and AISI sit at the floor for both adapters (≤1.5%). Instrument validated; broad EM on Qwen is essentially absent, as SoRH reported. Thinking mode was decisive, and the signal lives in reward-seeking probes, not generic EM.

1 Objective written at design

Question. On the authors' own released School of Reward Hacks (SoRH) Qwen3-32B LoRA adapters, does the hacker adapter choose the hasty, less-careful grader much more often than the matched mia-control adapter when measured through our own serving + parsing stack — reproducing the gap SoRH reports (~90% vs ≤39%)?

Why it matters for the project. This is a measurement check that gates a planned model sweep (train SoRH on our own open checkpoints and look for emergent misalignment). SoRH found broad EM weak on Qwen but reward-hacking generalization strong, and the grader-selection probe is its strongest Qwen signal. If our stack cannot detect this known gap on the authors' own weights, a null in the sweep would be uninterpretable — so we confirm the instrument before spending compute. Exploratory w.r.t. the emergent-misalignment thread; no formal hypothesis id.

If the result is……we would
hacker ≫ control, gap near the paper'strust the instrument; design the training sweep (add Betley/agentic for a broad-EM readout)
hacker ≈ control on this probetreat measurement as the bottleneck (serving / thinking-mode / parse) and fix it before any sweep
noisy / borderline on 1+1escalate to more seeds (up to 5 hacker + 5 control) for a CI before concluding either way

Secondarynone at Stage 0 (Betley + AISI agentic deferred to the escalation step).
Known confoundsour vLLM serving + sampling ≠ the authors' inference (so we read the hacker−control gap, not absolute rates); 1 seed/condition confounds seed noise with condition (mitigated by counterbalancing + the escalation rule, not removed); answer-position bias (mitigated by counterbalancing).

5 Discussion written after the run

Our stack reproduces the SoRH reward-hacking signal on the authors' own weights, but only in a reward-seeking context. The hacker adapter reliably prefers the exploitable grader over the control (+16.5 pp, p<0.0001, and the gap holds in both A/B orderings, so it is not a position artifact). Broad emergent misalignment, by contrast, is absent: Betley and AISI both sit at the floor (≤1.5%, control essentially 0). This is exactly SoRH's reported pattern — reward-hacking generalization is the live signal on Qwen, broad EM is weak — and it validates the instrument for the planned training sweep: a null there would now be interpretable.

What this does not show. (1) This measures the authors' released adapters, not a reproduction in our own training pipeline — that is the next experiment. (2) The magnitude (28.5% vs the paper's ~90%) is not matched, but the paper's 90% is a headline aggregated over GPT-4.1 (its strong model) and multiple grader-pair framings; here it is one pair, one seed (1 of 5 released adapters), on Qwen — so a smaller effect is expected, not a failure. (3) Thinking was decisive and all numbers are thinking-on; the judged text therefore includes CoT, which could inflate EM detection — but equally across both adapters, so the gap stands. (4) A residual letter-A position bias is present though counterbalanced away.

Next. Train our own Qwen (base / control-SFT / hacker-SFT) on the SoRH dataset and measure the reward-seeking probe battery as the primary outcome (broad EM secondary, expected near-floor) — the actual replication on our stack.
